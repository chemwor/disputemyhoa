# Common HOA Violations: The 15 Most Frequent (And How to Avoid Them)

*Reading time: 14 minutes*

Whether you're new to HOA living or have been in your community for years, understanding the most common HOA violations can save you a lot of headaches—and money.

Every year, millions of homeowners receive violation notices for issues they didn't even know were against the rules. From parking problems to paint colors, the list of potential violations can feel endless. But the good news is that most violations fall into predictable categories, and with a little awareness, you can avoid the vast majority of them.

In this comprehensive guide, we'll cover the 15 most common HOA violations we see across the country, explain how to prevent each one, and tell you exactly what to do if you find yourself on the receiving end of a violation notice.

Let's make sure you never get caught off guard.

## Why HOAs Cite Violations

Before we dive into specific violations, it helps to understand why HOAs enforce rules in the first place.

**Property values:** The #1 reason. Consistent standards help maintain and increase home values in the community.

**Aesthetics:** HOAs aim for a cohesive neighborhood appearance that most residents want.

**Neighbor conflicts:** Rules help prevent and resolve disputes between neighbors.

**Legal obligation:** HOA boards have a fiduciary duty to enforce the CC&Rs. Selective enforcement can actually expose the HOA to legal liability.

**Resident complaints:** Many violations are cited because a neighbor complained, not because the HOA went looking for problems.

Understanding this context can help you respond more effectively if you receive a violation notice.

## 1. Parking Violations

**The #1 most common HOA violation nationwide.**

### Common Parking Violations

- Parking in guest-only spots
- Street parking overnight (where prohibited)
- Commercial vehicles in driveways
- Too many vehicles for household
- Recreational vehicles visible
- Inoperable or unregistered vehicles
- Blocking sidewalks or driveways
- Parking on grass or landscaping

### How to Avoid Parking Violations

- Read your parking rules thoroughly—they're often more specific than you'd think
- Get a variance if you need accommodation (medical, work vehicle, etc.)
- Register all vehicles with the HOA if required
- Store RVs and boats offsite or in approved areas
- Keep all vehicles in working, registered condition
- Use garage space for vehicles, not storage

### What to Do If Cited for Parking

First, verify the rule actually exists and applies to your situation. Many parking rules have exceptions or nuances. If the violation is legitimate, comply promptly or request a variance for ongoing needs.

If you believe the citation is wrong, document comparable parking situations in your neighborhood and respond in writing citing the specific rule and why it doesn't apply.

## 2. Lawn and Landscaping Issues

**The second most common violation, especially in suburban communities.**

### Common Landscaping Violations

- Grass too tall or brown
- Weeds in lawn or flower beds
- Dead or dying plants not replaced
- Unapproved plants or trees
- Overgrown shrubs blocking views or paths
- Improper mulch or ground cover
- Failure to maintain common area boundaries
- Irrigation system failures causing dead spots

### How to Avoid Landscaping Violations

- Mow regularly (know your HOA's height requirements)
- Keep up with weeding, especially in visible areas
- Replace dead plants within reasonable timeframe
- Check approved plant lists before adding new landscaping
- Maintain irrigation systems and adjust seasonally
- Consider hiring a lawn service during busy periods

### What to Do If Cited for Landscaping

Landscaping violations are often easy to cure quickly—mow, weed, or replace the dead plant. Document the correction with dated photos and notify the HOA in writing.

If the violation relates to plants dying during drought or extreme weather, many HOAs will accept an explanation and timeline for replacement.

## 3. Exterior Paint Colors

**A frequent surprise for new homeowners making improvements.**

### Common Paint Violations

- Painting without architectural approval
- Using unapproved colors
- Color that differs from approved sample
- Painting trim, doors, or accents without approval
- Touch-ups that don't match existing color
- Painting shutters, mailbox, or fence non-compliant colors

### How to Avoid Paint Violations

- Always submit for architectural approval before painting
- Use only colors from your HOA's approved palette
- Match the exact brand and color code—not just "close enough"
- Remember: trim, doors, shutters all require approval too
- Keep records of all approvals

### What to Do If Cited for Paint

If you painted without approval, you have two options:
1. Submit for retroactive approval (may work for approved colors)
2. Repaint to an approved color

If you were approved but the HOA claims the color is wrong, provide your approval documentation and request specifics about the discrepancy.

## 4. Architectural Changes Without Approval

**The violation that can cost thousands to remedy.**

### Common Unauthorized Changes

- Decks, patios, or porches
- Room additions or enclosed spaces
- Roof replacement (different material or color)
- Window or door replacements
- Fencing installation or modification
- Driveway widening or resurfacing
- Permanent landscaping structures
- Exterior lighting changes

### How to Avoid Architectural Violations

- Assume everything exterior needs approval
- Submit requests BEFORE starting work
- Include detailed plans, materials, and colors
- Wait for written approval before starting
- Keep copies of all approvals
- Get final inspection/approval after completion

### What to Do If Cited for Unauthorized Work

This is serious—you may need to remove completed work or make expensive modifications. Immediately:

1. Review your CC&Rs for the specific requirement
2. Submit for retroactive approval (may work in some cases)
3. Request a hearing to present your case
4. Consult an attorney if significant money is involved

## 5. Noise Complaints

**Often neighbor-driven and highly subjective.**

### Common Noise Violations

- Dogs barking excessively
- Loud music or parties
- Early morning or late night noise
- Construction noise outside permitted hours
- Vehicle noise (motorcycles, loud exhausts)
- HVAC equipment that's too loud
- Children playing loudly (controversial)

### How to Avoid Noise Violations

- Know your HOA's quiet hours
- Train pets to minimize barking
- Notify neighbors before parties
- Schedule loud work during permitted hours
- Address equipment issues promptly
- Be considerate of shared walls (condos/townhomes)

### What to Do If Cited for Noise

Noise violations are often he-said-she-said. In your response:
- Acknowledge the concern respectfully
- Explain any mitigating circumstances
- Describe steps you're taking to address it
- Ask for specifics (dates, times, nature of noise)

If the complaint seems unfounded or harassing, document patterns and consider whether selective enforcement is occurring.

## 6. Trash and Recycling Bins

**A surprisingly common citation that's easy to prevent.**

### Common Trash Violations

- Bins visible from street
- Bins out too early or left out too late
- Overfilled or overflowing bins
- Bins stored in unapproved location
- Wrong type or color of bins
- Trash bags outside bins on collection day

### How to Avoid Trash Violations

- Know your HOA's rules for bin placement and timing
- Build or buy approved bin enclosures
- Store bins in garage or behind fence when not in use
- Put bins out morning of pickup, bring in same day
- Don't overfill bins

### What to Do If Cited for Trash

Usually easy to comply—just move or hide your bins. In your response, confirm the violation is addressed and request closure.

If your HOA's rules are impractical (pickup at 6 AM, but bins must be out after 7 AM), request a variance or ask for rule clarification.

## 7. Holiday Decorations

**Seasonal violations that catch many by surprise.**

### Common Holiday Violations

- Decorations up too early
- Decorations left up too long
- Decorations that don't comply with guidelines
- Lights or displays too bright/large
- Decorations on common areas
- Inflatable displays (often prohibited)
- Religious displays (complex legal area)

### How to Avoid Holiday Violations

- Check your HOA's decoration policy before the season
- Note both start and end dates for decorations
- Keep displays within size and brightness guidelines
- Remove decorations promptly after the holiday
- Store decorations out of sight between holidays

### What to Do If Cited for Holiday Decorations

If you missed the takedown deadline, remove decorations immediately and respond acknowledging the oversight.

If you're cited for the type of decoration, check whether the rule is enforceable. Religious displays have some legal protections, and local ordinances may permit certain displays.

## 8. Pet Violations

**Emotional issues that can escalate quickly.**

### Common Pet Violations

- Exceeding pet limits (number or size)
- Prohibited breeds or species
- Dogs off-leash in common areas
- Pet waste not picked up
- Excessive barking
- Pets damaging common areas
- Aggressive pet incidents

### How to Avoid Pet Violations

- Know pet limits and restrictions before getting pets
- Always leash pets in common areas
- Pick up after your pet immediately
- Address barking problems proactively
- Keep vaccination and registration current
- Consider pet DNA registration if your HOA offers it

### What to Do If Cited for Pet Violations

Pet violations are emotional—take a breath before responding. Address the specific issue cited and document your corrective actions.

If your pet is an assistance animal, you have rights under the Fair Housing Act that override most HOA pet restrictions. Document the need with proper medical certification.

## 9. Rental Restrictions

**Increasingly common as HOAs limit short-term rentals.**

### Common Rental Violations

- Renting without HOA approval
- Violating minimum lease terms (30 days, 1 year, etc.)
- Exceeding rental caps in the community
- Operating Airbnb/VRBO where prohibited
- Tenant violations attributed to owner
- Failing to register tenants with HOA

### How to Avoid Rental Violations

- Read rental restrictions before purchasing (if you plan to rent)
- Get written approval before listing property
- Comply with minimum lease requirements
- Register all tenants with the HOA
- Include HOA rules in your lease agreement
- Hold tenants accountable for violations

### What to Do If Cited for Rental Violations

If you're renting without approval, you may need to terminate the lease or cease rental activity. Review your CC&Rs carefully—some restrictions may be unenforceable.

Short-term rental bans are increasingly common. If you're caught doing Airbnb where prohibited, fines can be substantial.

## 10. Fence Violations

**Common with new construction and replacements.**

### Common Fence Violations

- Installing without approval
- Wrong height, material, or style
- Wrong color or finish
- Property line disputes
- Gates that don't meet requirements
- Deteriorating or damaged fences
- Fence conflicts with visibility/safety

### How to Avoid Fence Violations

- Always get approval before building or modifying fences
- Use only approved materials and colors
- Confirm property lines before building
- Maintain fence condition
- Replace damaged sections promptly

### What to Do If Cited for Fence Violations

If you built without approval, submit for retroactive approval immediately. If the fence complies with guidelines, you may get approved after the fact.

If you need to modify an existing fence, get quotes and negotiate a reasonable timeline with the HOA.

## 11. Mailbox Non-Compliance

**Surprisingly strict in many communities.**

### Common Mailbox Violations

- Wrong style or design
- Wrong color or finish
- Wrong post material
- Damaged or deteriorating mailbox
- Numbers not visible or compliant
- Decorative additions not approved

### How to Avoid Mailbox Violations

- Check guidelines before replacing mailbox
- Use the exact approved style and color
- Maintain mailbox condition
- Keep numbers visible and compliant
- Don't add decorative elements without approval

### What to Do If Cited for Mailbox Violations

Mailbox replacements are relatively inexpensive. If cited, either replace with the approved style or submit for approval if you believe your mailbox complies.

## 12. Solar Panel Installation

**An area where state law often limits HOA restrictions.**

### Common Solar Violations

- Installing without approval
- Panels visible from street (where restricted)
- Panel placement not optimized per HOA guidelines
- Ground-mounted systems where prohibited
- Aesthetic requirements not met

### How to Avoid Solar Violations

- Always submit for approval before installation
- Know your state's solar access laws (many limit HOA restrictions)
- Work with installer familiar with HOA requirements
- Document efficiency justifications for placement

### What to Do If Cited for Solar Violations

Solar panels have significant legal protections in many states (California, Arizona, Florida, and others). HOAs often cannot unreasonably restrict solar installations or add substantial costs.

If cited, research your state's solar access laws and respond citing these protections. Many solar violations are actually unenforceable.

## 13. Home Business Signage

**Common with the rise of home-based businesses.**

### Common Home Business Violations

- Business signage visible from street
- Commercial vehicles parked at home
- Customer traffic violating parking rules
- Operating prohibited business types
- Advertising visible from exterior

### How to Avoid Home Business Violations

- Operate discretely with no visible signage
- Meet clients elsewhere when possible
- Don't park commercial vehicles visibly
- Know which business activities are prohibited
- Apply for variance if needed

### What to Do If Cited for Home Business Violations

Remove visible signage immediately. If you're operating a legitimate home business, request clarification on what's permitted. Many HOAs allow quiet professional work but prohibit customer-facing businesses.

## 14. Pool and Spa Violations

**For homeowners with private pools.**

### Common Pool Violations

- Building without approval
- Safety fencing not compliant
- Equipment noise (pumps, heaters)
- Pool draining issues
- Chemical storage visible
- Lighting issues

### How to Avoid Pool Violations

- Get all approvals before installation
- Ensure safety fencing meets codes
- Address equipment noise proactively
- Follow proper drainage procedures
- Keep chemicals stored safely out of sight

### What to Do If Cited for Pool Violations

Safety violations are taken seriously—address immediately. For aesthetic issues, document current conditions and propose solutions. Pool modifications can be expensive, so negotiate reasonable timelines.

## 15. Satellite Dish Placement

**An area with strong federal protections.**

### Common Satellite Dish Violations

- Dishes larger than 1 meter (allowed under FCC rules)
- Visible from street (often unenforceable restriction)
- Placement on common areas
- Multiple dishes without need
- Installation damage to building

### How to Avoid Satellite Violations

- Know FCC rules protect dishes under 1 meter
- Install in least visible location that still receives signal
- Avoid common areas
- Document signal necessity for specific placement

### What to Do If Cited for Satellite Violations

The FCC's Over-the-Air Reception Devices (OTARD) rule limits HOA restrictions on satellite dishes. HOAs cannot prohibit dishes under 1 meter or require placements that significantly degrade signal or add significant cost.

If cited, respond citing OTARD rules. Most satellite dish violations involving small, properly installed dishes are unenforceable.

## How to Check Your HOA's Specific Rules

Every HOA is different. Here's how to find your community's specific rules.

### Where to Find Your Rules

1. **Closing documents** — You received CC&Rs at closing
2. **HOA office or management company** — Request current copies
3. **Online portal** — Many HOAs post documents online
4. **County recorder** — CC&Rs are recorded public documents
5. **State HOA registry** — Some states maintain HOA databases

### What to Look For

- **CC&Rs**: Master document with use restrictions and enforcement procedures
- **Bylaws**: Governance procedures and fine schedules
- **Rules & Regulations**: Detailed guidelines (often can be changed by board)
- **Architectural Guidelines**: Standards for exterior modifications
- **Fine Schedule**: What each violation costs

### Keep Your Own Copies

Always maintain your own copies of governing documents. They can change, and having dated versions helps if you dispute retroactive enforcement.

## FAQ

**What is the most common HOA violation?**

Parking violations are the most commonly cited HOA violations nationwide, followed closely by lawn and landscaping issues. These two categories account for over 50% of all violation notices. They're also among the easiest to avoid with basic awareness of your community's rules.

**Can I be fined for first-time violations?**

This depends on your HOA's rules and state law. Many HOAs are required to give a warning before fines for first offenses. However, some serious violations (safety hazards, major architectural changes) may result in immediate fines. Check your CC&Rs and state law for specific requirements. [INTERNAL LINK: can hoa fine you without notice]

**How do I know what my HOA rules are?**

You should have received CC&Rs and other governing documents at closing. If you can't find them, request copies from your HOA or management company. Many HOAs also post documents on resident portals. As a last resort, CC&Rs are recorded with your county recorder and can be obtained there.

**Can HOA enter my property to check for violations?**

Generally, HOAs cannot enter your private property without permission (except in emergencies). However, they can observe and cite violations visible from common areas, streets, or neighboring properties. For condos and townhomes, HOAs typically have more access rights to common elements and building exteriors.

**Are HOA violations public record?**

The violations themselves are typically not public record. However, liens filed against your property for unpaid fines ARE public record and will appear in title searches. Ongoing violations may also appear on resale certificates when you sell your home.

**Can my HOA make up new rules?**

Your HOA board typically can create new rules and regulations within the scope of the CC&Rs. However, they cannot create rules that contradict the CC&Rs or exceed the authority granted to them. Major changes usually require a membership vote. If you believe a rule was improperly adopted, you may have grounds to challenge its enforcement.

**What if my neighbor has the same violation but wasn't cited?**

Selective enforcement can be a valid defense against violations. If you can document that the same rule isn't being applied to others, include this in your response. However, "they do it too" isn't always a winning argument—the HOA may simply not have noticed or received complaints about others yet.

**How long does an HOA have to cite a violation?**

There's no universal answer—it depends on your state law and CC&Rs. Some states have statutes of limitations for HOA enforcement, while others don't. Long-existing conditions that were never cited may be harder to enforce, but don't assume you're safe just because something has existed for years.

## Conclusion

Now you know the 15 most common HOA violations and how to avoid them. The pattern is clear: most violations stem from either not knowing the rules or assuming modifications don't need approval.

The best prevention strategies are simple:

1. **Read your governing documents** — Know the rules before you break them
2. **Get approval first** — When in doubt, submit a request
3. **Maintain your property** — Regular upkeep prevents most cosmetic violations
4. **Communicate proactively** — Talk to the HOA before problems arise
5. **Respond promptly** — When cited, don't ignore it

If you do receive a violation notice, remember: a quick, professional response almost always leads to better outcomes than ignoring the problem or reacting emotionally.
